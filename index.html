REAP

Learning General Rearrangement Heuristics from Motion-Planner Feedback

Daniel Swoboda, Hector Geffner

Chair of Machine Learning and Reasoning, RWTH Aachen University

Conference on Robot Learning (CoRL) 2026

REAP learns a Q-value function that scores symbolic actions by anticipating which will be geometrically feasible. The function can drive a greedy policy or guide best-first search at deployment. Because it rarely proposes actions the motion planner rejects, REAP needs roughly an order of magnitude fewer motion-planner queries than classical and learning-based baselines*, and the same recipe carries over to five robot rearrangement domains.

*Excluding BT-h, which is given the object ordering of a reference plan and only refines placements; it is not a like-for-like comparison.

Abstract.

Robotic rearrangement tasks require planning sequences of actions that fulfill high-level specifications while also being geometrically feasible. Symbolic abstractions make the combinatorial structure of rearrangement tasks tractable, but they usually do not fully capture the geometric constraints that determine which actions a robot can actually realize. This is due to the complexity of expressing the underlying geometric feasibility constraints in the symbolic model. We propose REAP (Rearrangement with Elicited Action Preconditions), a framework in which a relational graph neural network heuristic is learned over a simple symbolic abstraction. A motion planner serves as a supervisor that elicits the hidden geometric preconditions of the problem during training. Deployed with greedy best-first search, the learned heuristic generalizes out-of-distribution along object count, plan length, and object arrangement. Compared to classical heuristic search, TAMP, and learning-based baselines, REAP solves more problems, and requires roughly an order of magnitude fewer motion-planner queries than comparable learning-based methods. REAP anticipates geometric feasibility, suggesting geometrically feasible actions in 98% of instances. We show that the same architecture, training recipe, and search procedure adapt to various robotic rearrangement domains, with cross-domain generalization and transfer capabilities.

Method.

01

Coarse symbolic abstraction

Each environment is encoded as a coarse PDDL/STRIPS planning problem with a small set of predicates (cell occupancy, gripper state, support relations) and action schemas for picking and placing. The abstraction is deliberately a loose over-approximation: on open-tabletop, 80% of the symbolically applicable actions in the initial states are geometrically infeasible, typically because a neighbouring cylinder blocks the gripper’s side approach. REAP’s contribution is learning to predict which ones without consulting the motion planner.

02

Learned action-selection policy

A relational GNN maps state-action pairs (s, a) to scalar Q-values, the estimated number of remaining actions to the goal, and outputs them for all applicable actions in a single pass. Taken together, the Q-values define a policy that selects actions while anticipating their geometric feasibility. States and goals are encoded as atoms, so a single architecture generalizes across instances with different numbers of objects and cells.

03

Motion-planner-as-supervisor

During training, a weighted-A* search (w = 2) that expands a random frontier node with probability ε collects rollouts and consults a motion planner for the feasibility of each expanded action. Infeasible actions incur a dead-end cost, so the learned heuristic anticipates which symbolically applicable actions will fail the geometric check. Early in training these dead-end signals dominate the learning signal.

04

Deployment via greedy best-first search

At test time, a greedy best-first search uses the learned Q-values to order successors and consults the motion planner only for the actions it expands. Feasibility verdicts are cached and reused on later visits. A purely greedy variant, REAP-greedy, commits to the lowest-cost action at every step without backtracking.

Comparison with Baselines.

On the open-tabletop suite, REAP-GBFS solves substantially more problems than the classical search baselines (BrFS, hFF), an integrated TAMP planner (PDDLStream), and three learning-based methods we reimplemented against the same action space and the same feasibility oracle — SAHS, PLOI and BT-h — while making roughly an order of magnitude fewer motion-planner queries per solved plan.

The comparison against SAHS is the sharpest: it searches about as efficiently as REAP (1.31 versus 1.27 expansions per plan step), so the two explore the symbolic space equally well. The entire difference is what gets proposed — and whether the motion planner accepts it.

PDDLStream shows a sharper failure mode: it solves ~98% of instances that need no relocation, but collapses to ~13% once two to four objects must be relocated and to zero beyond that. The pattern holds for all four configurations we evaluate.

Out-of-Distribution Generalization.

REAP’s learned heuristic generalizes out-of-distribution along several axes. On open-tabletop, the policy trained on at most 14 objects with plan lengths up to 11 reaches 95.9% solve rate under REAP-GBFS across the full 300-problem suite (up to 24 objects, plan lengths up to 15). REAP-GBFS stays at or above 89% on every object-count, plan-length and object-arrangement bucket that contains more than two problems per seed, while the greedy variant degrades on longer plans and harder arrangements.

Open-Tabletop: three OOD axes

Cross-environment OOD generalization

A single goal-conditioned policy solves Access-19 in distribution but none of the 18-blocker instances. Splitting the task into two policies, one that extracts the target and one that restores the blockers, and running them in sequence solves 25 of 33 held-out layouts with 12–18 blockers, including five 17-blocker layouts and the 18-blocker benchmark instance (a 78-step plan, found in under 45 s), when trained on up to 17-blocker problems.

Joint training and transfer

One policy trained jointly on open-tabletop, confined-shelf and multi-level shelf solves 96.3% of the 300 open-tabletop problems, all 250 confined-shelf problems, and 94 of 100 multi-level shelf problems, so REAP can be trained on several environments at once.

Limitations.

REAP is evaluated entirely in simulation: sim-feasible plans transfer to a real arm only insofar as the simulated kinematics and geometry are faithful, and the symbolic state is assumed provided rather than perceived. Each new environment requires its own simulator, a feasibility oracle supplying per-action verdicts, and a training problem distribution. Per-action motion-planner queries during training rollouts dominate cost, around 24 GPU-hours per run. The action space is restricted to prehensile placements on a discrete grid, leaving non-prehensile manipulation, contact-rich interaction, and articulated-object handling out of scope. The clearest failure we observe is environments that require subgoal decomposition: on Access-19 the task requires moving blockers away from their goal positions before restoring them, and two policies trained one per phase and executed in sequence do solve it, although the split into phases is designed by hand rather than learned.
